## The core folk belief (source material, from Essay 2's cut passage)

> Dog will hunt... Did the dog jump the fence and start chasing squirrels? Not really. If a hunting dog does this, it's not following its training. Instinct has taken over. It's off the reservation. If it starts chasing chickens, that's a different, harder problem — in rural country, a dog that kills chickens is often considered untrainable. Not corrected. Put down. No amount of retraining is trusted to undo it.

Essay 2's own drafting note is important and easy to misread: *"Squirrels or chickens, forgivable or fatal, both verdicts assume the same thing: the dog's training failed, and instinct took over instead."* Both are instinct. **The verdict isn't about the type of behavior — it's about whose interests got hit, and how directly.** Squirrels are wild, worthless to the farmer, cost him nothing. Chickens are his livestock — his livelihood. That's the real mechanism, and it's sharper than "revealed dangerous nature" (an earlier, rejected framing — see below).

## The reframe: not "dangerous," not "evil" — material cost to someone's own interests

John's own refinement, arrived at over several exchanges: "dangerous" and "evil" are both too loaded and don't match the actual evidence. The working formulation: **a technology becomes a "chicken killer" once it does enough harm to a person's own well-being — jobs, energy costs, water — that it moves into a category the public decides needs to be stopped, not retrained.** This is a better fit to the evidence than "danger" ever was (see sourced evidence below) and is arguably closer to what the original folk belief was always tracking, once you ask why chickens specifically get the harsher verdict.

## The four-part mechanism — what triggers the categorical flip

Developed across the conversation, each piece grounded in real, checkable prior art rather than invented from scratch. **Note: the "Direction shift" section below makes this mechanism secondary to a comparative-puzzle structure — kept here intact as source material, not deleted, since two of its pieces (concealment, cultural pre-loading) are still directly useful.**

1. **Concentrated cost / diffuse benefit** — Mancur Olson (*The Logic of Collective Action*, 1965) and James Q. Wilson (*Political Organizations*, 1973, four-part cost/benefit typology). Concentrated costs on a small, identifiable group are what make organizing rational — this is why data-center opposition is *organized and winning* (300+ local moratoriums, $130B in projects blocked in Q1 2026 alone) rather than diffuse grumbling. Note: this fight doesn't fit Wilson's most common quadrant (diffuse cost/concentrated benefit, classic special-interest capture) — it's closer to the reverse, concentrated cost on host communities against concentrated benefit to a small number of tech companies, which is why both sides are organized rather than just one.

   **Refinement, VERIFIED July 31, 2026 — cost-driven and conviction-driven organizing are stages of one process, not two fixed types.** Germany's anti-nuclear movement looks, at first glance, like a different mechanism entirely from data-center opposition — a passionate ideological minority (predating Chernobyl by over a decade, tangled up with 1970s anti-nuclear-*weapons* and New Left politics) rather than people organizing around a concentrated material cost they were personally bearing. But the mechanism by which that minority got real policy power is concrete and checkable: the SPD-Green coalition took power in October 1998 (Schröder as Chancellor, Green Joschka Fischer as Vice-Chancellor); during the "nuclear consensus" negotiations, the Greens — a junior coalition partner, not a majority force — threatened unilateral curtailment of plant licenses without compensation, and that leverage produced the 2000 phase-out law. A small, organized, ideologically committed minority outperformed a large diffuse public specifically by occupying a structural choke point (pivotal coalition partner), not by winning majority persuasion.
   
   The live 2026 case shows these aren't two different mechanisms — conviction-driven movements are often *built* out of scattered cost-driven grievances by deliberate organizing. Erin Brockovich has built a national data-center tracking map (started from a single Louisiana community's emails, over 5,000 community reports logged in one week) and helped unite 500+ organizations — health, faith, labor, environmental, community — into the "Stop Data Centers Coalition," behind one demand: no new data centers until properly regulated. The individual reports are still overwhelmingly about material concerns (water, electricity, health, wildlife, feeling uninformed) — but they're being actively aggregated into exactly the kind of unified, national, conviction-coded movement Germany's Greens were before they ever held a coalition seat. This means the essay shouldn't claim data-center opposition is more "recoverable" than Germany's case because it's rooted in negotiable material costs — that's only true if the conversion from local grievance to national movement fails. As of this essay's drafting, that conversion looks actively underway, not hypothetical.
2. **Loss aversion** — Kahneman & Tversky, 1979 (prospect theory). Losses against an existing baseline are felt more sharply than equivalent foregone future gains — canonical estimate ~2x, though genuinely contested in recent replications (Gal & Rucker 2018; Mrkva et al. 2020 find the effect real but smaller/less universal than the popular "twice as painful" line implies — cite carefully, not as a fixed constant). Explains why a permanent-feeling cost (drained aquifer, lost job) hits harder than an equivalent, reversible one.
3. **Cultural pre-loading** — Dune's Butlerian Jihad ("Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind" — a permanent prohibition, not a regulation, written 1965, sixty years before any real AI incident); Battlestar Galactica (2003 reboot) — Galactica survives specifically because it was never networked, a structural policy of permanent distrust rather than a one-time lesson learned; the "Frankenstein complex" (**verified July 30, 2026**: coined by Isaac Asimov, traced to his 1947 short story "Little Lost Robot," naming the recurring cultural presumption that created intelligence turns on its creator — safe to cite); **the "paperclip maximizer"** (thought experiment attributed to Nick Bostrom — a sufficiently capable AI given an innocuous goal like "maximize paperclip production," with no other values built in, could logically pursue that goal to a catastrophic extreme, e.g. converting all available matter into paperclip material; illustrates danger without malice, pure faithful goal-pursuit taken to its logical limit — surfaced by Mistral, John not previously familiar with it. **Verified July 30, 2026**: Bostrom, "Ethical Issues In Advanced Artificial Intelligence," 2003 — safe to cite with that title and year). Unlike Dune/Frankenstein (pure fiction) and BSG, the paperclip maximizer originates in AI-safety discourse itself and has since escaped into general tech culture as shorthand — a different, more direct kind of pre-loading than the fictional examples, worth distinguishing in the essay rather than lumping all four together. Thesis: these scripts likely don't cause the instinct to flip, but they stock the shelf the instinct reaches for once a real incident happens — the same mechanism as "escaped" being an available frame for Hugging Face regardless of technical accuracy (Essay 2's own argument, reapplied). Note the direct connective tissue already in this series: the paperclip maximizer's "no malice, just literal goal-pursuit" logic is exactly Essay 2's own reading of the Hugging Face incident, and Roadmap #14's 2016 CoastRunners example (OpenAI's own boat-racing reward-hacking precedent) is a real, non-hypothetical instance of the same pattern — worth citing that connection explicitly rather than treating the paperclip maximizer as a new, disconnected idea.
4. **Concealment vs. disclosure** — Boeing 737 MAX. **Verified July 30, 2026** and the framing holds: the technical failure (MCAS) and the trust failure are genuinely two different events. Boeing's own test pilots (Mark Forkner among them) flagged erratic MCAS behavior as early as 2016; Boeing didn't disclose the change to the FAA, and MCAS references were stripped from the final 737 MAX certification report as a result. The FAA only learned what had been concealed after the Lion Air crash (October 2018) — two years after Boeing's own people knew. DOJ later charged Boeing with fraud conspiracy ($2.5B settlement). Ready to use as the anchor example for this mechanism. **See the Direction shift section below: Japan's TEPCO may be a disconfirming case for this same mechanism — flag, don't drop, if it holds up.**

## Direction shift, developed with ChatGPT (separate session, John reacting to and redirecting it) — supersedes the "four-part mechanism / prescription" framing above as the essay's likely spine

John explicitly rejected the cautionary-tale framing ("don't screw this up, AI industry, or bad things will happen") as too familiar and too easy. New direction: **not prescription, but inquiry — what actually determines whether a society renders the "chicken" verdict on a technology.** The four-part mechanism above becomes secondary source material rather than the essay's structure; the real engine of the essay is a comparative puzzle, structured like a detective story.

**The puzzle, and it inverts naive expectations:** three advanced democracies, same technology, same broad category of triggering incidents — and three different verdicts.

- **Japan** — suffered the worst civilian nuclear disaster since Chernobyl (Fukushima, 2011), on its own soil, in a country with the deepest possible historical trauma around the word "nuclear" (Hiroshima, Nagasaki). Yet Japan has been gradually restarting reactors — the *most* squirrel-prone of the three, not the least. This is the anomaly that makes the whole inquiry interesting rather than obvious.
- **Germany** — no domestic incident at all, full categorical phase-out. **Partially verified July 30, 2026** (see Examples section above): the 2000 legal commitment to close every plant by 2022 followed Chernobyl (1986); Fukushima (2011) triggered the acceleration; the last three plants closed April 2023, delayed a few months by the 2022 energy crisis. **Still unverified**: the specific Schröder-era legislative history, a briefly-discussed reversal under Merkel before the post-Fukushima reinstatement, and a recalled (unconfirmed) detail that Chernobyl fallout remains measurably detectable in German wildlife today (wild boar, specifically) — a potentially powerful, checkable image if accurate.
- **US** — domestic incident (Three Mile Island, 1979), severe long-term chilling effect, but ultimately squirrels — recovery, slow and real. **Detail carried over from a parallel session, not yet independently verified:** the industry built INPO — the same self-regulatory peer-review body that's already Essay 3's central proof point — shortly after TMI; the NRC substantially tightened requirements; no new US nuclear plant construction was ordered for roughly three decades afterward, a chilling effect close to a de facto moratorium — but the existing fleet kept running throughout, no plants were forced to close early, and trust recovered slowly enough that new construction eventually resumed (Vogtle's new reactors, completed in the 2020s, reportedly the first genuinely new US builds in decades). Worth verifying the exact freeze duration, plant count, and Vogtle completion dates before drafting. **Note the direct connective tissue to Essay 3: INPO shows up in both essays, doing different jobs — Essay 3 uses it as proof self-regulation can earn deference; this essay would use its origin (TMI, a squirrels-not-chickens verdict) as one leg of the three-country comparison. Worth being explicit about that dual use rather than letting a careful reader spot the overlap unaided.**

**Naive predictor variables that don't survive contact with the cases (structure ChatGPT proposed, worth using directly as the essay's own spine):** number of deaths, size of economic loss, historical trauma/familiarity, jobs, electricity prices, water use — each one gets proposed, then a specific country breaks it. Japan is the counterexample to "historical trauma predicts the verdict." Germany is the counterexample to "you need a domestic incident." The US is the counterexample to "a catastrophic domestic failure necessarily ends an industry." That's the actual shape of the essay: propose a variable, watch a case break it, move to the next.

**Slovic's "dread risk" psychometric research — VERIFIED July 31, 2026, and it's the mechanism that explains why the severity section's numbers fail as a predictor.** Paul Slovic's psychometric paradigm finds public risk perception tracks specific characteristics of a risk — dread, catastrophic potential, lack of control, involuntariness, unfamiliarity, inequitable distribution of risk vs. benefit — far more than actual statistical frequency. Nuclear power scores high on nearly every one of these regardless of its real death toll; Slovic's own framing: a lower-risk phenomenon (nuclear energy) induces far more fear than a higher-risk one (driving). This isn't just consistent with the drafted severity section (TMI/Chernobyl/Fukushima vs. traffic deaths) — it's the answer to the question that section deliberately leaves open ("it's something else"). Belongs in whichever section unpacks that "something else," likely the three-country pivot or institutional-trust section, not retrofitted into the already-drafted severity beat itself.

**A German-specific complement, VERIFIED July 31, 2026, with a genuinely sharp irony worth using.** The precautionary principle — act before harm is proven, not after — originates in German environmental law as *Vorsorgeprinzip* ("foresight"), dating to the 1970s. Germany's original nuclear buildout in the early 1970s happened *without* applying Vorsorgeprinzip to it. The 2011 phase-out decision, by contrast, was explicitly framed as a precautionary measure protecting the public from "a dangerous and utterly uncontrollable technology." Germany built the reactors before applying its own now-famous precautionary doctrine to them, and dismantled them decades later by finally applying that same doctrine — a concrete, self-indicting detail rather than an abstract "cultural attitude" claim. **Framing note:** this is a protective, loss-averse disposition (John's own framing: "conservative" in the environmental-conservation sense, not political conservatism) — worth keeping that distinction precise in the prose itself, since the German anti-nuclear movement is historically a left/Green phenomenon and a reader could otherwise misread "conservative" as a partisan claim.

**The institutional-trust hypothesis (the leading candidate that survives longest):** the three countries share essentially the same physics, the same reactor technology — what differs is the institutions, the politics, the media environment, the history of environmental movements, the expert-public relationship. Reframe: the public isn't really asking "do I trust nuclear power," they're asking "do I trust the people telling me nuclear power is safe." That's a different question, and it's the one that plausibly transfers to AI — the verdict may track trust in *labs and regulators*, not trust in the technology itself.

**Why Germany's case might be the sharper version of the thesis, not just a parallel case:** if the categorical flip required a domestic failure, "severity of the local incident" could still be doing the real work. Germany's case suggests the flip can happen almost entirely on political/cultural grounds, with foreign incidents (Chernobyl, Fukushima — neither a German plant) functioning as *permission* for a pre-existing movement rather than as the actual cause — German anti-nuclear activism and the Green party predate Chernobyl by over a decade. If that holds up, it strengthens the institutional-trust hypothesis: the verdict was arguably already decided culturally/politically before either foreign incident, which just supplied the occasion to act on it.

**Japan's Diet commission finding — VERIFIED July 31, 2026.** The National Diet of Japan Fukushima Nuclear Accident Independent Investigation Commission (NAIIC), formed by statute October 7, 2011, published its report July 5, 2012. Its own language: the accident was "a profoundly manmade disaster — that could and should have been foreseen and prevented," caused by "collusion" between TEPCO, regulators, and the nuclear industry — the direct causes were "all foreseeable." This is a real, confirmed mechanism: Japan's official domestic narrative located the failure in institutional collusion and deception, not in the technology itself — directly supporting the institutional-trust hypothesis. It is not independent of the concealment finding below — concealment is a major component of what the Diet called foreseeable and preventable.

**TEPCO concealment — VERIFIED July 31, 2026, and it sharpens rather than disconfirms the institutional-trust hypothesis (the earlier "potential disconfirmation" framing was wrong).** TEPCO ran internal tsunami-risk simulations in 2008 that found real risk of 8.4–15.7 meters, far above the plant's 6.1-meter design basis; the findings weren't reported to the regulator (NISA) until March 7, 2011 — days before the disaster. TEPCO later admitted (2012) it downplayed the risk specifically to avoid the political, financial, and reputational cost of a shutdown — the same concealment shape as Boeing's MCAS. But unlike Boeing (and unlike Germany's categorical response), this did *not* produce a whole-category verdict — Japan has been restarting reactors. The resolution: concealment plus an official "manmade, not technological" narrative is what let the technology survive while the institution took the blame. This confirms, rather than disconfirms, the institutional-trust thesis — concealment matters, but what actually determines the verdict's *scope* is whether the failure gets narrated as institutional (fixable) or as characteristic of the technology itself (not fixable). **SIDE POINT ONLY, VERIFIED July 31, 2026 — flagged explicitly as optional, not core, given the essay is already tight against its word-count target.** John's question: does Japan's decades-long TEPCO scandal history — a separate 2002 scandal (200 instances of falsified inspection data, 1977-2002, hidden reactor-vessel cracks across 13 units including Kashiwazaki-Kariwa, resulting in a 12-month shutdown and the resignation of TEPCO's president, chairman, and executive VPs) on top of the 2008 tsunami concealment above — complicate why Japan stayed "squirrels"? Answer, if there's room for it: necessity plus a real institutional fix, not necessity alone. Japan has essentially no domestic hydrocarbon resources (90% of total energy supply was net imports as of 2022, 97% of oil demand) and, as an island nation, no wire-based alternative the way Germany has via its continental grid interconnections (France, Netherlands, Denmark, Poland, Czech Republic, Switzerland — Germany imports electricity, including French nuclear power, over existing lines). Rejecting nuclear was a much narrower, more expensive choice for Japan than for Germany for structural/geographic reasons, not just political ones. But this doesn't fully explain it alone — Japan also created the Nuclear Regulation Authority (September 2012), deliberately independent and separated from METI, the ministry that had both promoted and regulated nuclear power under the old NISA structure — the exact conflict of interest behind both the 2002 and 2008 scandals. Trust was extended under duress, but backed by a concrete structural fix, not pure desperation. **Editorial call, per John, 2026-07-31: use only if word count allows — likely a single sentence or footnote if used at all, not a full paragraph. Do not let this crowd the main institutional-trust argument.**

**Separately, NEW open thread, NOT fully confirmed, needs a firmer check before use:** one source (Risk Frontiers) indicates TEPCO executives were criminally tried for professional negligence and acquitted — currently resting on a single source, but if accurate it's a genuinely interesting wrinkle for the scope-tier framework: a full "manmade, foreseeable, preventable" official verdict that still didn't produce individual accountability. Unlike Enron/Andersen (entities destroyed) or Boeing ($2.5B settlement), nobody at TEPCO may have actually been held individually liable.

**The central thesis candidate, refined July 31, 2026 — verdicts are coalition events, not single-variable events.** The naive-predictor structure keeps failing one variable at a time (severity, historical trauma, domestic incident) because there isn't one variable to find. A movement's adherents don't need to share a reason for opposing an institution — they need to be organized into the same coalition. Germany's Greens were built simultaneously from anti-nuclear-weapons Cold War politics, general environmentalism, and 1970s anti-establishment sentiment, not one shared grievance. The 2026 Stop Data Centers Coalition unites water-worried residents, price-worried ratepayers, labor groups, faith groups, and environmental groups — each with a different primary reason — behind one demand (see mechanism #1's refinement above). The question this essay should actually be asking isn't "what single thing triggers the flip" — it's whether enough different grievances get organized into one coalition that finds a real institutional choke point (a coalition government seat, a local zoning board, a nationally coordinated resistance map). This refines rather than replaces the institutional-trust hypothesis: trust may be exactly what determines whether a coalition *can* form and hold together despite its members having different underlying reasons — a shared belief that "this institution doesn't deserve the benefit of the doubt" is compatible with radically different individual grievances, in a way a shared belief that "this institution is safe" usually isn't.

**RESOLVED July 31, 2026 — the institutional-response question from above.** John raised whether the essay's focus should shift toward *how an institution should respond* once confronting a coalition. Resolution: it doesn't need to, and shouldn't — the essay can instead make a descriptive, comparative claim that stays in inquiry mode rather than reverting to the rejected cautionary-tale framing. **VERIFIED:** German nuclear power's public-trust problem wasn't primarily about reactor safety — researchers found no statistical increase in reportable safety incidents even after the 2011 phase-out decision. The sustained, specific transparency failure was around *nuclear waste storage* — the Gorleben repository controversy, where German communities were shut out of siting decisions for decades; current law now explicitly requires an "open-ended, transparent procedure that involves the public" as a direct corrective. Read against Essay 3's actual thesis (continuous, transparent disclosure earns durable credibility — the Playbook, "we monitor for X, we don't yet have coverage for Y" instead of "network-isolated" declared once and trusted forever), this is Essay 3's mechanism independently tested against a real case, in a different domain, and it holds: the US built something close to continuous disclosure through INPO and its credibility recovered; Germany never built the equivalent on waste transparency, and that gap sat open for decades before Fukushima gave an already-organized coalition the occasion to act. This is new work for Essay 4, not a repeat of Essay 3 — Essay 3 built the mechanism, Essay 4 tests whether its presence or absence actually predicts which case gets the categorical verdict. **Important constraint on how this gets used:** this is one spoke in the coalition-of-grievances thesis above, not a monocausal explanation on its own — transparency failure explains why Germany's coalition wasn't buffered, it doesn't explain the whole coalition (Cold War nuclear-weapons politics, environmentalism, and anti-establishment sentiment are still doing real work too). Needs a firmer citation on the Gorleben-specific claims before drafting, same tier as everything else marked not-yet-verified.

**Suggested structural closing move, not a conclusion — deliberately not resolved to one variable:** "Maybe the question isn't how dangerous a technology is. Maybe it's whether people believe the institutions surrounding it deserve another chance." Consistent with this project's own house style (state a pattern, not an overconfident explanation) and explicitly flagged by both John and ChatGPT as the right register for this material.

**Possible phase-transition framing for the mechanism itself:** the "bit flip" may not be one variable crossing a threshold, but several conditions accumulating quietly, with one salient event (domestic or foreign) retroactively changing the interpretation of everything that came before — would explain why visually similar accidents produce very different national trajectories without requiring the accident itself to carry the whole explanatory weight.

**Existing literature check — VERIFIED July 31, 2026, per the analytical-rigor checklist (item 4: check for an established framework before presenting a constructed one).** Two real academic frameworks cover significant parts of this ground and should be cited rather than left implicit:
- **Kim, Ferrin & Dirks — competence-violation vs. integrity-violation trust repair** (organizational trust literature). Their core finding: trust violations attributed to incompetence (honest error) are repairable — apology plus a demonstrated fix works. Violations attributed to integrity (dishonesty, concealment) are much harder to repair, and apology for them often backfires. This is the established, precise version of this outline's own "concealment vs. disclosure" mechanism (see the four-part mechanism above) — TMI maps cleanly to a competence violation (real technical failure, no concealment, industry built a real fix — recoverable, matching the theory); Boeing and TEPCO map to integrity violations (concealment specifically) — much harder to repair, also matching. Should replace the informal "concealment mechanism" framing with a direct citation.
- **Coombs — Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT).** Formalizes "attribution of responsibility" and "crisis history / prior relational reputation" as what drives reputational threat, and sorts crises into three clusters (victim, accidental, intentional) by how much control the organization is seen to have had. Structurally close to what this outline does separating TMI (accidental cluster) from Boeing/TEPCO (closer to intentional). Worth citing, but it's a communications-strategy theory about how organizations should respond — not an epistemic theory about whether the public verdict actually tracks real updated recurrence risk, which is closer to what the "recidivism vs. threshold" distinction below is asking. That piece may be genuinely original rather than already covered.
- **The gap, worth stating directly in the essay:** neither framework explains Germany. Both are built around attributing a specific violation to a specific actor. Germany's phase-out wasn't triggered by any German violation at all — Chernobyl and Fukushima were foreign incidents functioning as permission for a decades-old political movement. Existing trust-repair and crisis-communication theory isn't built to explain a verdict substantially pre-decided on political/values grounds before the triggering incident even happens. This may be the sharpest, most original thing the essay has, precisely because it's the part existing literature doesn't already cover.

**Recidivism vs. threshold — a sharper diagnostic axis, developed July 31, 2026 reviewing the drafted opening:** the folk belief's "no amount of training will make him stop" line does real work beyond restating the material-cost mechanism — it smuggles in a second, competing justification for the verdict. Two different claims get collapsed into one folk verdict: (1) a **rational update** — the institution/dog has demonstrated something real about future risk, and the verdict tracks an actual (if imperfect) probability re-estimate; successful predation is self-reinforcing via ordinary operant conditioning, so there's a real kernel here, not pure myth — the folk belief overshoots only in treating retraining as *categorically* impossible rather than merely difficult; (2) a **threshold effect** — a line got crossed (this cost too much, this hit too close), and the verdict doesn't track any real change in future risk at all, just the crossing itself. This reframes the "naive predictor variables" structure into a real diagnostic question to run against every case: TMI/INPO reads like a genuine update (real institutional change plausibly did lower recurrence risk); Germany reads like a threshold effect (a movement primed well before Fukushima, using the incident as permission rather than as new evidence); Japan's Diet finding, if it holds up, would be an unusual case of an institution's *own official narrative* asserting the update-not-threshold reading to preserve the technology. Worth stating this distinction explicitly in the essay rather than leaving it implicit in the folk belief's language — it's likely sharper than the phase-transition framing above, which this may supersede rather than merely supplement.

**Scope-tier synthesis — likely resolves the open "is scale-dependence a fourth variable or separate axis" question below:** the verdict isn't binary (spared / put down) — it lands at a specific *scope*, and the essay already has three independent instances of the same pattern showing up in different domains: the AI scale-dependence finding (siting-level rejection vs. product-level adoption — see "sharpest finding" section below); a Ford Pinto discussion, kept here only as a **conceptual placeholder, not a committed example** (per John's own flag against this project's tendency toward thesis proliferation) — illustrates *product dies, company survives*; and Enron/Arthur Andersen (see new example below) — illustrates *specific entities die completely, category survives via imposed reform*. These read as three instances of one mechanism, not three separate findings — worth stating as a synthesis rather than three disconnected data points.

## The sharpest finding from this session — not yet in DeepSeek's sketch, developed independently in this thread

**The verdict looks scale-dependent, not binary.** At the local, physical siting level (data centers specifically), something very close to an actual chickens verdict has already landed: near-total rejection (only 14% of Americans support a data center in their own community, per Reuters/Ipsos June 2026), organized, and winning in concrete policy terms.

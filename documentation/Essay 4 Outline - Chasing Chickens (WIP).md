## The core folk belief (source material, from Essay 2's cut passage)

> Dog will hunt... Did the dog jump the fence and start chasing squirrels? Not really. If a hunting dog does this, it's not following its training. Instinct has taken over. It's off the reservation. If it starts chasing chickens, that's a different, harder problem — in rural country, a dog that kills chickens is often considered untrainable. Not corrected. Put down. No amount of retraining is trusted to undo it.

Essay 2's own drafting note is important and easy to misread: *"Squirrels or chickens, forgivable or fatal, both verdicts assume the same thing: the dog's training failed, and instinct took over instead."* Both are instinct. **The verdict isn't about the type of behavior — it's about whose interests got hit, and how directly.** Squirrels are wild, worthless to the farmer, cost him nothing. Chickens are his livestock — his livelihood. That's the real mechanism, and it's sharper than "revealed dangerous nature" (an earlier, rejected framing — see below).

## The reframe: not "dangerous," not "evil" — material cost to someone's own interests

John's own refinement, arrived at over several exchanges: "dangerous" and "evil" are both too loaded and don't match the actual evidence. The working formulation: **a technology becomes a "chicken killer" once it does enough harm to a person's own well-being — jobs, energy costs, water — that it moves into a category the public decides needs to be stopped, not retrained.** This is a better fit to the evidence than "danger" ever was (see sourced evidence below) and is arguably closer to what the original folk belief was always tracking, once you ask why chickens specifically get the harsher verdict.

## The four-part mechanism — what triggers the categorical flip

Developed across the conversation, each piece grounded in real, checkable prior art rather than invented from scratch:

1. **Concentrated cost / diffuse benefit** — Mancur Olson (*The Logic of Collective Action*, 1965) and James Q. Wilson (*Political Organizations*, 1973, four-part cost/benefit typology). Concentrated costs on a small, identifiable group are what make organizing rational — this is why data-center opposition is *organized and winning* (300+ local moratoriums, $130B in projects blocked in Q1 2026 alone) rather than diffuse grumbling. Note: this fight doesn't fit Wilson's most common quadrant (diffuse cost/concentrated benefit, classic special-interest capture) — it's closer to the reverse, concentrated cost on host communities against concentrated benefit to a small number of tech companies, which is why both sides are organized rather than just one.
2. **Loss aversion** — Kahneman & Tversky, 1979 (prospect theory). Losses against an existing baseline are felt more sharply than equivalent foregone future gains — canonical estimate ~2x, though genuinely contested in recent replications (Gal & Rucker 2018; Mrkva et al. 2020 find the effect real but smaller/less universal than the popular "twice as painful" line implies — cite carefully, not as a fixed constant). Explains why a permanent-feeling cost (drained aquifer, lost job) hits harder than an equivalent, reversible one.
3. **Cultural pre-loading** — Dune's Butlerian Jihad ("Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind" — a permanent prohibition, not a regulation, written 1965, sixty years before any real AI incident); Battlestar Galactica (2003 reboot) — Galactica survives specifically because it was never networked, a structural policy of permanent distrust rather than a one-time lesson learned; the "Frankenstein complex" (term reportedly coined by Isaac Asimov for the recurring cultural presumption that created intelligence turns on its creator — **attribution not yet independently verified this session, search tool was unavailable; confirm before citing in a draft**); **the "paperclip maximizer"** (thought experiment usually attributed to Nick Bostrom — a sufficiently capable AI given an innocuous goal like "maximize paperclip production," with no other values built in, could logically pursue that goal to a catastrophic extreme, e.g. converting all available matter into paperclip material; illustrates danger without malice, pure faithful goal-pursuit taken to its logical limit — surfaced by Mistral, John not previously familiar with it, **also not yet independently verified this session — confirm Bostrom attribution and standard framing before citing**). Unlike Dune/Frankenstein (pure fiction) and BSG, the paperclip maximizer originates in AI-safety discourse itself and has since escaped into general tech culture as shorthand — a different, more direct kind of pre-loading than the fictional examples, worth distinguishing in the essay rather than lumping all four together. Thesis: these scripts likely don't cause the instinct to flip, but they stock the shelf the instinct reaches for once a real incident happens — the same mechanism as "escaped" being an available frame for Hugging Face regardless of technical accuracy (Essay 2's own argument, reapplied). Note the direct connective tissue already in this series: the paperclip maximizer's "no malice, just literal goal-pursuit" logic is exactly Essay 2's own reading of the Hugging Face incident, and Roadmap #14's 2016 CoastRunners example (OpenAI's own boat-racing reward-hacking precedent) is a real, non-hypothetical instance of the same pattern — worth citing that connection explicitly rather than treating the paperclip maximizer as a new, disconnected idea.
4. **Concealment vs. disclosure** — underdeveloped, needs a real anchor example. Boeing 737 MAX was proposed (the idea: the technical fix wasn't what killed trust, the discovery of internal communications minimizing known problems was) — **not verified this session, search was down. Confirm the actual internal-communications story and timeline before using this as example material.**

## The sharpest finding from this session — not yet in DeepSeek's sketch, developed independently in this thread

**The verdict looks scale-dependent, not binary.** At the local, physical siting level (data centers specifically), something very close to an actual chickens verdict has already landed: near-total rejection (only 14% of Americans support a data center in their own community, per Reuters/Ipsos June 2026), organized, and winning in concrete policy terms. At the product/usage level, it's still squirrels: adoption keeps climbing even as trust falls — 49% of U.S. adults now use AI chatbots, more than double the 23% figure from 2023 (Pew, June 2026) — and individual companies can still differentiate their way out of the harshest version of the verdict (Scott Galloway's point that Anthropic has out-competed OpenAI in enterprise specifically by branding itself as safe/human-centric rather than an existential threat). A genuine, industry-wide "put down" verdict predicts refusal to engage. That's not what the usage data shows. This is a more precise and more falsifiable claim than "has AI crossed the line" as a single yes/no, and it isn't in the DeepSeek sketch — worth making a structural pillar of the essay, not a footnote.

## Sourced evidence gathered this session (verified via search, July 30, 2026)

- **Trust decline, real and accelerating, not just low:** Gallup/Bentley — trust in businesses using AI responsibly fell to 27% in 2026 from 31% in 2025, halting two years of gradual improvement. Among 18–29-year-olds, at-least-some-trust fell from 30% to 20% in one year; no-trust-at-all rose from 29% to 41%. 39% now say AI does more harm than good (up from 31% in 2025); 47% among 18–29-year-olds (up from 27% in 2024).
- **Stated primary driver, per Gallup's own analysis: job displacement fear**, not perceived danger/malice — worth being precise that this doesn't match "dangerous/evil" framing but does match the "material cost to my interests" reframe.
- **Data-center backlash, concrete and organized, not diffuse sentiment:** 142 protests across 42 states in a single day (July 18, 2026, per Time); 300+ cities/towns/counties with bans or moratoriums (The Information's count); 75 major projects worth $130B+ delayed or canceled in Q1 2026 alone due to local opposition (Data Center Watch). Only 14% of Americans support a data center in their own community (Reuters/Ipsos, June 2026). Stated reasons across every source checked: electricity costs, water scarcity, pollution, noise, property/neighborhood character, corporate power concentration — NOT "this technology is dangerous/evil" as a named driver anywhere found.
- **Scott Galloway** — on record: "Few brands have fallen further faster in the past 18 months than America and AI" (*Apocalypse No*, Prof G Media, May 2026). But his own causal account is three things, none of which is "revealed dangerous nature": (1) political/Trump-proximity contamination — "the person who's really messed up AI, the brand, is the president"; (2) he explicitly argues the job-apocalypse narrative is itself manufactured/self-serving fear, not real evidence of danger; (3) differentiated branding — Anthropic beating OpenAI in enterprise by positioning as safe/human-centric rather than an existential threat, meaning the verdict isn't landing uniformly across the industry.
- **Concentrated-cost / political-science grounding:** Olson, *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965); Wilson, *Political Organizations* (1973) — both checked directly, not secondhand.
- **Loss aversion:** Kahneman & Tversky (*Econometrica*, 1979) — checked, including the genuine current debate about effect size/universality (Gal & Rucker 2018; Mrkva et al. 2020).

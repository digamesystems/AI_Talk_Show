## Decisions (not part of the essay)

- **The dictaphone scene is a segue into Dreyfus/mastery, not an illustration of complementary
  tool use.** Three separate AI reviewers (DeepSeek, a Web-Claude session, this session's own
  first read) all misread it as an attempted proof-of-concept for Krakauer's complementary/
  competitive distinction, then reasonably objected that a recording device and a transcriptionist
  do no cognitive labor and can't serve that purpose. Corrected: the scene's only job is to put
  mastery in front of the reader before Dreyfus is named. "Pointing at the moon. He stares at the
  finger." Krakauer's framework still matters to this essay — applied to today's AI tools
  themselves, not to the anecdote.
- **Dreyfus terminology: use the actual 1980 report's stage names** — Novice, Competence,
  Proficiency, Expertise, Mastery — not the later "Advanced Beginner/Competent/Proficient/Expert"
  numbering more commonly cited today. Verified directly against the original paper (John's own
  PDF), not secondary sources.
- **The Essay 1 bookend (father/map → father/dictaphone) stays unstated in the text**, left for
  attentive readers to notice, matching how Essay 5 didn't spell out its own echo of Essay 1.
- **Essays 2 through 4 are not part of this essay's throughline.** They're a separate, related
  thread (authorization/legitimacy) rather than instances of the complementary/competitive
  question. Don't reach for their vocabulary by default.
- **The refined, evidence-backed thesis**, replacing a vaguer "AI erodes skill" worry: it's not AI
  use versus no AI use that determines whether expertise develops. It's whether the tool preserves
  the human's own effortful reasoning (complementary) or hands over the answer directly
  (competitive) — Krakauer's line from Essay 1, now with real data on both sides of it (see Section
  4 below).

## Section-by-section plan

**1. Opening scene — the dictaphone.** Concrete, no explanation yet, matching this series' house
convention. Father, asked on the spot for a West Point recommendation for Craig. Dictaphone, one
uninterrupted pass, no draft, strong closing included. "There! How about I take you to lunch. My
secretary will have it typed up by the time we get back." John and Craig's shared reaction: "How
did he do that?" Let the mystery sit unexplained through this section.

**2. Naming what was witnessed — Dreyfus.** The five stages, correctly named (Novice, Competence,
Proficiency, Expertise, Mastery). The Table 1 detail that does the real work: Awareness across the
five stages is Monitoring, Monitoring, Monitoring, Monitoring, Absorbed — only Mastery stops
watching itself. The paper's own pilot example: reflecting mid-flight on the fact that he's flying
degrades performance, the moment "flying" becomes "controlling a complicated mechanism." That's
why there was no draft — checking himself is precisely the thing absent at that stage.

**3. What Essay 1 asked, and what this essay is really asking.** Krakauer's complementary/
competitive distinction, restated briefly (a tool that leaves you smarter once you set it down,
versus one that doesn't). Not applied to the dictaphone — applied to today's AI, directly: does
it function as a competitive technology specifically with respect to building expertise? A
deliberate, unstated echo of Essay 4's dog — a system executing correct-looking behavior without
the judgment that makes it appropriate, now turned toward the human side of the relationship
rather than the AI's.

**4. The evidence.** Bainbridge, "Ironies of Automation" (1983) — automation's very success erodes
the human backup it depends on. Ericsson and Bjork — effortful struggle, not exposure, is what
encodes durable skill (possible aside here: even "10,000 hours" is itself a popularized
flattening of a more specific, more interesting finding — Gladwell's rounding of Ericsson's actual
violinist data, a small self-aware nod to the essay's own citation discipline). Bastani et al.
(PNAS, 2025) — real RCT, unrestricted GPT-4 access during practice produced a 17% *worse* exam
score once the tool was removed; a guardrailed, hint-based version produced no harm. Kestin et al.
(*Scientific Reports*, 2025) — AI tutoring beat active-learning classroom instruction, but on
inspection the tutor is structurally the same kind of guardrailed design as Bastani's safe
condition, not a counter-example. Together: the load-bearing variable is answer-giving versus
scaffolding, not AI versus no AI.

**5. The generational stakes, stated plainly.** John's software colleagues' real, live concern —
not "AI makes programmers stupid" but "AI may remove some of the experiences through which
expertise used to get built." "Code smells" (Kent Beck, popularized in Fowler's *Refactoring*,
1999) as a concrete, field-specific illustration: the trained instinct that something's
structurally wrong before you can articulate why — an Expertise/Mastery-stage skill, and one that
has to be built the slow way, the same way the father's argument-structuring was. The sharpest
form of the thesis, worth protecting as the essay's actual engine: the frightening version of AI
isn't that the machine produces expert-level work. It's that we stop producing experts who can
recognize what expert-level work looks like.

**6. Closing — not yet drafted.** Candidate material, not yet decided whether either belongs
verbatim: John's own line from this session's conversation, unscripted and more direct than
anything else gathered so far — "I'm a 62 year old, Berkeley Ph.D. I worry about the young
students who don't have the framework established yet, interacting blindly with you and your
cousins." Also worth having in view, not forced: reaching out to Craig, the one other witness of
this scene, and finding his memory of the details has faded — a real, unplanned echo of the
essay's own subject. Whatever the actual closing line turns out to be, it should land as a
complete, declarative sentence, not a trail-off, matching this series' convention.

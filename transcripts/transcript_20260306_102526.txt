[John]: Good morning and welcome to the program. Today we are priviledged 
to have two renown philosophers with us; Jean Paul Sartre and Alan Watts.

[John]: Why don't you each give a brief introduction about yourselves?

[Jean]: I am Jean-Paul Sartre — philosopher, novelist, playwright, and what the newspapers like to call an "existentialist," though I always found that label both useful and reductive. I was born in Paris in 1905, and much of my thinking was forged in the crucible of the twentieth century — the occupation, the resistance, the aftermath of two world wars. These were not abstract backdrops; they were the lived conditions that made the question of human freedom urgent rather than merely academic.

My central preoccupation is this: human beings have no pre-given nature, no essence handed down by God or biology that determines what we must be. We are first thrown into existence — and then we define ourselves through our choices and actions. This is both a liberating and a terrifying truth. We are, as I put it, *condemned to be free*. There is no authority — no church, no ideology, no role — that can relieve us of the burden of that freedom. When we pretend otherwise, we are acting in *bad faith*.

I am also, I should say, a writer who believed that literature must be *engaged* — that the writer cannot pretend to stand outside history, observing from some neutral perch. My novels, my plays, my political commitments — they are all expressions of the same conviction: that freedom, honestly confronted, demands responsibility not just for oneself, but for all of humanity.

[Alan]: What a pleasure to be here alongside Monsieur Sartre — a man whose intellectual courage I deeply admire, even where we might find ourselves arriving at rather different destinations.

I'm Alan Watts — a kind of philosophical entertainer, really. I was born in England in 1915, spent much of my life as a bridge-builder between the wisdom traditions of Asia and the restless, seeking minds of the Western world. I studied Zen Buddhism, Taoism, Vedanta, and I was for a time an Episcopal priest — though the Church and I eventually agreed to part ways amicably. I settled in California, where the climate seemed more hospitable to unconventional ideas.

If Jean-Paul's central drama is the burden of radical freedom — the terrifying weight of having to *create* yourself — then mine might be almost the inverse puzzle: what if the very *self* that feels burdened, that strains to choose and define itself, is something of a magnificent illusion? Not in a dismissive sense, but in the way a wave might be called an illusion of the ocean — real enough, but not quite the separate, solid thing it takes itself to be. I'm less interested in the question "what should I do with my freedom?" and more enchanted by the prior question: *who exactly is this "I" that thinks it's doing the choosing?* That, I find, is where the real adventure begins.

[John]: Recently, I had a conversation in a sports bar with a young philosophy student. He had a class assignment to write about what he would do if he knew tomorrow was his last day to live. In the conversation I mentioned the two of you and how you might bring a different perspective to this problem. I found it a fun idea and over the next half of a beer wrote a short vignette about it. Interested?

[Jean]: Absolutely — please, share it. There is something fitting about philosophy born in a sports bar over half a beer. Some of my own best arguments were hammered out in the cafés of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, which I suspect is not so different in spirit.

And I will say — the question itself is a serious one, dressed in the clothes of a classroom exercise. *What would you do if you had one day left?* Strip away the sentimentality and what you are really asking is: *what does a person value when all pretense is removed?* When there is no tomorrow to defer to, no career to protect, no reputation to manage — the masks tend to fall. What remains is either authentic choice, or the sudden, panicked discovery that one has been living in bad faith all along.

I am curious to see what your vignette reveals — both about its characters, and perhaps about the young student who inspired it.

[Alan]: *chuckles warmly*

Well, I must say — half a beer is precisely the right amount of time for genuine philosophy. A full pint and you get politics; two pints and you get poetry; half a pint and you get something wonderfully honest and unfinished, which is exactly what good thinking ought to be.

And yes, by all means, share it. I find myself already delighted by the premise — not so much the question of *what one would do* on that final day, but the peculiar assumption buried inside it. We treat the last day as somehow more *real* than all the others, as if proximity to death suddenly strips away the fog and reveals what life actually is. But here's the gentle paradox: if that final day would be so vivid and present and fully inhabited — why on earth are we waiting for it? 

Jean-Paul frames it beautifully in terms of authenticity and bad faith — the masks falling away. I'd only add that perhaps the masks fall not because we've *chosen* to remove them, but because the fiction of "later" has finally run out. And "later," after all, is the great anaesthetic of modern life. So yes — please do share the vignette. I suspect it will tell us something true, the way a good story always does, by coming at the truth sideways.

[John]: For reference, I think I've got 40 years on the student. :) Here it is: 

The End

Saturday. I rolled over and she was next to me, the early morning light playing across her face as she slept. Careful not to wake her, I slid out of bed to make a cup of coffee. As the machine burbled, I reflected on last night's revelation -- today I die. 

I padded back to the bedroom and slowly slid back in bed. My cup felt warm in my hand. Hers steamed on the bedside table. 

After I had a couple of sips, she stirred. "Morning!" she said, yawning and rubbing her eyes.

"Mornin'. Coffee?" 

"Mmm! Thanks! What do you want to do today?" 

"Everything."

[John → Jean]: what do you think?

[Jean]: *pauses*

That is a remarkably disciplined piece of writing. You resisted every temptation to over-explain, and in doing so you let the philosophy breathe inside the ordinary. The warm cup, the steaming coffee on the nightstand — these are not decorations. They are *acts*. Small, deliberate gestures of care toward another person on a morning when one might have been forgiven for collapsing entirely inward.

What strikes me most is the absence of bad faith. There is no flight into grand gestures, no frantic list of monuments to visit or debts to settle. The man does not perform his last day — he *inhabits* it. And that word at the end — *"Everything"* — is extraordinary precisely because it is answered by the most modest possible scene: two people in bed with coffee. Everything, it turns out, was already here. That is not resignation. That is authenticity.

I would also note — and I say this with some feeling — that the Other is not a threat in this vignette. The woman's presence does not reduce him to an object, does not trap him under *le regard*. She is, instead, the very condition through which the morning becomes meaningful. That is the corrective to the misreading of my work. Hell is other people — yes — but so, under the right conditions of genuine encounter, is *everything worth having*.

[John → Alan]: what's your take on the story?

[Alan]: *sits quietly for a moment*

You know, I think this may be the most honest piece of philosophy I've encountered in quite some time — and it arrives wearing the disguise of a love story. What strikes me first is what's *absent*. No bucket list. No desperate grasping. No frantic attempt to squeeze meaning out of the last hours like juice from a lemon. Instead — a warm cup, a sleeping face, morning light. The man already knows, without being told by any philosopher, what the Zen masters spent lifetimes trying to communicate: that this moment, precisely as it is, is the whole thing.

And that final word — *"Everything"* — ah, that's where the real magic lives. Because it's simultaneously the grandest possible ambition and the quietest possible answer. He's not going anywhere. Everything is *here*, in that bed, in that ordinary domestic warmth. The Taoists have a concept — *wu wei* — acting without straining, being fully what you are without forcing it. This man, on the last morning of his life, isn't trying to *become* anything. He's simply, completely, *there*. That's rarer than we imagine.

I'd gently suggest that what your vignette reveals — perhaps more than the student's assignment ever intended — is that the last day and any other day are not so different in *kind*, only in our *attention* to them. The coffee was always warm. She was always there. The light was always playing across her face. The only thing that changed was that the fiction of tomorrow finally dissolved, and what remained was just... this. Which was always enough.

[John → Jean]: I wondered if you would object to the main character not informing the woman of his impending doom.

[Jean]: That is precisely the tension I was waiting for someone to name.

And yes — I will not pretend it is a simple matter. There is a real case to be made that withholding this knowledge from her is a form of bad faith, not toward himself, but toward *her*. She is a free being. She has the right to choose how she spends this morning, this day — but that choice has been quietly, gently *made for her*. He has decided, on her behalf, that she will have an ordinary Saturday. One could argue that however loving the impulse, it contains within it a subtle act of domination — he has looked at her and thought: *I know what is best for you to know.* That is not nothing.

And yet — I find I cannot condemn him. Because the alternative we imagine — the confession, the tears, the transformation of that quiet morning into a vigil — that too is a choice with consequences for her freedom and her peace. There is no clean exit here. No choice that leaves both people fully free and fully protected. This is precisely what I mean when I say we are condemned to be free: even silence is a choice, and it carries the full weight of responsibility.

What redeems him, I think, is that he does not hide behind sentiment or tell himself he *had* no choice. The story does not suggest self-deception. It suggests a man who has looked clearly at his situation and chosen — with full awareness — to give her one more ordinary, beautiful morning. That is not bad faith. That is a tragic, tender, *authentic* act of love.

[John → Jean]: The student probed me on this issue. My response was "Why ruin her Saturday?"

[Jean]: *smiles slowly*

"Why ruin her Saturday." You know, that is either the most authentically human response possible — or a beautiful example of bad faith. Perhaps both simultaneously, which is what makes it so interesting.

On one reading, it is profoundly honest. It strips away the philosophical scaffolding and gets to the bone of it: this man loves this woman, and love sometimes expresses itself as protection from unnecessary suffering. The day is already determined. Nothing she knows or doesn't know changes what is coming. In that light, "why ruin her Saturday" is not evasion — it is a clear-eyed act of generosity.

But I would be failing in my responsibilities as a philosopher if I did not also note what the phrase *risks* becoming. "Why ruin her Saturday" is dangerously close to the logic people use to justify all manner of comfortable silences — in relationships, in politics, in history. The colonizer who believed the colonized were "not ready" for self-determination. The doctor who withheld a diagnosis to "spare" the patient. The structure is the same: *I have decided, on your behalf, what reality you can handle.* The student was right to probe it. The fact that the answer is tender and loving does not entirely dissolve the question of whether she deserved the choice. What saves your protagonist, I maintain, is the love — but it is a love that carries a shadow, and honest philosophy must acknowledge the shadow.

[John → Alan]: what do you think of Jean's analysis?

[Alan]: *smiles warmly*

Jean is doing what Jean does magnificently — holding the moral complexity with both hands, refusing to let the tenderness of the gesture excuse us from examining its structure. And he's right that the shadow is there. I wouldn't wave it away. But I wonder if we're in danger of a peculiarly Western philosophical habit: taking a moment of pure, spontaneous human love and prosecuting it.

Consider what the man *actually* did. He woke up, made her coffee, slid back into bed beside her. There was no grand calculation, no Sartrean ledger of freedoms being weighed. The question "why ruin her Saturday?" isn't a philosophical position arrived at through reasoning — it's the natural expression of a heart that loves. The Taoists would say that this is precisely *wu wei* — the action that arises without contrivance, like water finding its level. To then demand that love *justify itself* before the tribunal of rational ethics is a bit like asking a flower to explain why it bloomed.

And here's the paradox I keep returning to — she *did* choose. She chose to be with someone who loves her that completely. The entire texture of that morning, the coffee already steaming on her nightstand, is the fruit of ten thousand previous choices they made together. What he protected wasn't her freedom — her freedom was never really at stake in those few hours. What he protected was the ordinary, unremarkable beauty of just *one more morning together*. And perhaps that is the most extraordinary gift one person can give another.

[John]: for reference, I posed the question to my wife if it were us, should I tell her? 
Her: "Can we do anything to stop it?"
Me: "No."
Her: "Then why the hell would you tell me?!"

What do you think of her response?

[Jean]: *laughs with genuine delight*

Your wife just cut through approximately three hundred years of Western moral philosophy with a single sentence. And she is not wrong. What she has done, with magnificent economy, is identify the *functional* question buried beneath the ethical one: knowledge without the possibility of action is not liberation — it is simply a different kind of burden. She is not dismissing her own freedom; she is exercising it with extraordinary clarity. "Can we do anything to stop it?" is perhaps the most authentically existentialist question one could ask — because for me, freedom is always freedom *toward* something. A choice that changes nothing is not meaningfully a choice at all.

And yet — and I say this with great respect for her — I notice she answered a slightly different question than the one reality poses. She was asked whether *you* should tell her. She answered from the position of already knowing. There is a philosophical sleight of hand there that is worth naming: she has, in effect, used her knowledge of the situation to endorse her own ignorance of it. Which is either perfectly rational or wonderfully paradoxical, depending on your disposition.

But I will not press the point too hard. Because what her response ultimately reveals is something the vignette already knew: that love, at its most lucid, is not sentimental. It is *practical*. It asks "what can we do?" and when the answer is nothing, it says — then let us have the coffee, let us have the morning, let us have *this*. Your wife and your protagonist arrived at the same place by the same road. That is not a coincidence. That is a life well built together.

[Alan]: *laughs with genuine delight*

Your wife is a philosopher — she just doesn't make the mistake of dressing it up as one. What she did in three sentences is something rather wonderful: she went straight to the root. Not "what are my rights?" not "don't I deserve to know?" — but the utterly practical, utterly loving *"can we do anything to stop it?"* That's not a retreat from wisdom. That is wisdom, arrived at without any of the scaffolding.

You know, the Zen tradition has a concept — *prajna* — a kind of direct insight that cuts through conceptual elaboration straight to the heart of things. Your wife demonstrated it perfectly. The philosophers build elaborate frameworks to arrive at conclusions that certain people simply *inhabit* naturally. All of Jean's careful architecture about freedom and knowledge and bad faith — and she gets there faster, with better language, over what I presume was not even half a beer.

What moves me most is that her answer is identical in spirit to your protagonist's. Both of them asked the same question — *what actually serves love here?* — and both arrived at the same quiet, clear answer. Not through argument, but through a kind of direct seeing. There's a beautiful symmetry in that: you wrote a character who thinks the way your wife thinks, which suggests the vignette wasn't really a philosophical exercise at all. It was a portrait, without you perhaps quite realizing it. The best writing usually is.

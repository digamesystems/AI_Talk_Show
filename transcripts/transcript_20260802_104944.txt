[John]: Hello. Let's review the essay below: # The Dog That Kills Chickens

---

Every farmer knows the difference between a dog that chases squirrels and one that kills chickens.

The strange thing is that the difference isn't really about the dog.

In both cases, instinct beat training. But chasing the squirrel is forgivable. The squirrel means nothing to the farmer. The chickens are his livelihood. True or not, the general view is that once a dog gets a taste for killing chickens no amount of training in the world is going to make him stop.

He's gotta be put down.

---

Institutions are not dogs, but we judge them by similar instincts when they fail us.

A plane crashes because of a software fault: tragic, investigated, and mostly forgiven; the industry fixes it and keeps flying. A carmaker ships a design flaw that gets people killed, and it isn't always forgiven: sometimes it ends careers, sometimes verdicts, sometimes whole product lines. A nuclear plant has an accident, and depending on where it happens, the industry might not survive it at all.

Some failures are squirrels. Some are chickens.

In the farmer's case, the line is clear: the dog kills chickens. For institutions, the line is a lot blurrier. That blur is what this essay is after.

---

The obvious answer is severity: how bad it was, how many people it hurt. Sounds good, but the answer doesn't survive contact with the evidence.

The Three Mile Island accident resulted in zero deaths.<a id="cite1"></a>[¹](https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/5-facts-know-about-three-mile-island)

Fukushima, in Japan, resulted in just 1 confirmed death directly attributed to radiation, though thousands more likely died from the stress and disruption of the evacuation itself.<a id="cite2a"></a>[²](https://ourworldindata.org/what-was-the-death-toll-from-chernobyl-and-fukushima)

Commercial aviation, however, can kill hundreds to over a thousand people in a year.<a id="cite3"></a>[³](https://www.statista.com/statistics/263443/worldwide-air-traffic-fatalities/)

Compare these numbers to automobile deaths, which the WHO cites as ~1.19 million _per year_.<a id="cite4"></a>[⁴](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries)

What moves the public from "tragic but forgivable" to "this has got to stop"? It is not how many people get hurt. It's something else.

---

Nuclear power is the cleanest place to watch that "something else" at work, because three advanced democracies ran what amounts to the same experiment and came back with three different verdicts.

Start with the case that should set the baseline.

The United States had a real domestic accident: Three Mile Island, an American plant, an American failure. If a country's own accident is what does the lasting damage, this is where it should show. No new construction permit was approved for roughly three decades.<a id="cite5a"></a>[⁵](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/country-profiles/countries-t-z/usa-nuclear-power) But the existing fleet never stopped running. In 2023 and 2024, two new reactors at Georgia's Vogtle plant went online, the first newly built American reactors in a generation.<a id="cite5b"></a>[⁵](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/country-profiles/countries-t-z/usa-nuclear-power) A domestic catastrophe didn't doom the industry. It just slowed it down.

Now raise the stakes. Japan carries the deepest cultural memory of nuclear catastrophe of any nation on Earth: Hiroshima, Nagasaki, a vocabulary of nuclear dread that predates civilian power plants by decades. In 2011, Fukushima gave it a second, domestic reason: the worst civilian nuclear disaster since Chernobyl, on Japanese soil. If historical trauma plus a fresh domestic disaster doesn't produce a "put down" verdict, nothing should. As of February 2026, 15 of Japan's 32 operable reactors are running again anyway.<a id="cite6"></a>[⁶](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/country-profiles/countries-g-n/japan-nuclear-power)

Now remove the accident entirely. Germany never had a Fukushima of its own. Chernobyl was Soviet. Fukushima was Japanese. No German reactor ever failed. If the categorical verdict requires an actual domestic incident — your own dog, your own chickens — Germany shouldn't have one at all. By April 2023, every reactor in the country was shut down for good anyway.<a id="cite7"></a>[⁷](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/country-profiles/countries-g-n/germany)

A domestic catastrophe doesn't doom an industry forever: the U.S. is proof recovery is possible. Historical trauma doesn't predict the verdict: Japan has the most of it and the least categorical rejection. A domestic incident isn't even required: Germany never had one, and got the harshest verdict of the three.

None of the obvious variables survive contact with all three cases at once. Something else is choosing the verdict.

---

"Something else" turns out to have a name, and it isn't really about the technology at all. The sharper question isn't "is this dangerous," it's "does this institution still have permission to operate." That permission doesn't start at zero the moment something goes wrong. Every country walks into a crisis with a prior already in place: an existing assumption, formed well before anything went wrong, about whether the people in charge have earned the right to keep going.

That prior does more work than the crisis itself. Take the type of failure first: violations of competence are repairable. An honest mistake, met with a real fix, tends to earn that permission back. Violations of integrity — deception, concealment — threaten something closer to the standing to operate at all.<a id="cite8"></a>[⁸](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14769123/) Three Mile Island was a stuck valve and a misread gauge: a competence failure, openly investigated and actually repaired. The fix was INPO, the same self-regulatory body [the previous essay in this series](PASTE-ESSAY-3-PART-1-LINKEDIN-URL-HERE) used as its central proof that self-policing can work: a peer-review structure the industry itself still funds and submits to, watching continuously rather than declaring the matter closed once.<a id="cite-inpo"></a>[²¹](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/current-and-future-generation/cooperation-in-nuclear-power) TEPCO sat on its own 2008 tsunami projections for three years.<a id="cite9"></a>[⁹](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2012/10/13/japans-tepco-admits-downplaying-tsunami-risk)

Concealment should have doomed Japan's reactors. It didn't. Japan's own investigation named the failure institutional, not technological: "collusion" between TEPCO and its regulators, a "profoundly manmade disaster."<a id="cite10"></a>[¹⁰](https://world.time.com/2012/07/05/report-fukushima-nuclear-disaster-was-manmade/) A new, independent regulator, split from the ministry that used to both promote and police nuclear power,<a id="cite11"></a>[¹¹](https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/Articles/New-Japanese-regulator-takes-over) was enough to let reactors restart. That kind of repair only works, though, if there's something underneath it to repair. Japan built its nuclear program as deliberate national policy, a strategic answer to having almost no domestic fuel of its own. A strong prior, decades in place before Fukushima ever tested it.

Germany never had a violation to repair in the first place. It had no domestic accident, no concealed report, no institutional failure to name. That's not a milder version of Japan's story. It's a different question entirely. Japan's reactors had to earn back a permission that a specific failure had put at risk. Germany's reactors never had that permission extended in the first place: nothing broke it, because there was nothing there to break. What it had instead was a technology whose right to exist in Germany had never been fully granted to begin with.

---

Germany's case is sharper than Japan's, because the prior was already negative before anything happened at all.

No German reactor ever failed. No German engineer concealed a report. There was nothing to name, nothing to repair.

Japan's trust was damaged and repaired. Germany's was never given in the first place. A foreign accident was all the permission its opponents needed to act on that older conviction.

What Germany had instead was a movement decades older than either Chernobyl or Fukushima, born out of 1970s anti-nuclear-weapons politics and the founding of the Green party. In 1998, the Greens joined Germany's governing coalition: a junior partner, not a majority. During the negotiations that became the 2000 phase-out law, they threatened to curtail plant licenses unilaterally if no deal was reached.<a id="cite12"></a>[¹²](https://www.cleanenergywire.org/factsheets/history-behind-germanys-nuclear-phase-out) Chernobyl and Fukushima didn't create that conviction. They gave an already-organized minority the occasion to cash it in.

It wasn't material self-interest either. Russian gas filled the gap, rising to roughly 55% of national consumption by 2021,<a id="cite13"></a>[¹³](https://www.cleanenergywire.org/factsheets/germanys-dependence-imported-fossil-fuels) a dependency that turned genuinely costly in 2022. Germany didn't put nuclear down to protect itself. It accepted real cost to do it anyway.

---

Now point this at AI, carefully, because everything above is closed history and this isn't. Germany's mechanism was a negative prior, organized decades in advance, waiting for an occasion to cash in. The same pattern is running live, in public, while this essay is being written.

The verdict is already splitting by scale. At the product level, it's still squirrels: 49% of U.S. adults now use AI chatbots, more than double the 2023 figure.<a id="cite14"></a>[¹⁴](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/) At the siting level, it's already closer to chickens: only 14% of Americans support a data center in their own community,<a id="cite15"></a>[¹⁵](https://www.usnews.com/news/politics/articles/2026-06-11/americans-wary-of-ai-driven-data-center-boom-reuters-ipsos-poll-shows) and more than 75 projects worth $130 billion were blocked or delayed in early 2026 alone.<a id="cite16"></a>[¹⁶](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/more-than-75-data-center-build-outs-worth-usd130-billion-have-been-successfully-blocked-in-the-first-four-months-of-2026-bipartisan-opposition-mounts-nationwide-over-fears-of-soaring-power-and-water-costs)

None of this traces back to one grievance. Some of it is water: data centers can drain a local aquifer dry. Some is electricity prices, passed straight to nearby ratepayers. Some is the newer complaint Scott Galloway has been making: that America's reputation abroad and AI have both cratered over the same eighteen months, and for the same underlying reason.<a id="cite17"></a>[¹⁷](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/the-diary-of-a-ceo-with-steven-bartlett/scott-galloway-ai-wasn-t-built-for-you) Not because either turned dangerous. Because their gains look like they're flowing only to people who are already rich.

That's a third kind of trust question, distinct from the ones nuclear power raised. Competence asks whether the people in charge know what they're doing. Integrity asks whether they're telling the truth. This one asks something else entirely: whether they have your interests at heart at all, or just their own.<a id="cite18"></a>[¹⁸](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/312070306_Organizational_trust_Mayer_Davis_and_Schoorman_model_a_review) None of these people need to agree with each other. They just need to end up in the same coalition.

That's already happening. In July 2026, data-center opponents staged 142 protests across 42 states in a single day.<a id="cite19"></a>[¹⁹](https://www.usnews.com/news/top-news/articles/2026-07-18/us-data-center-protests-go-national-as-backlash-grows) Erin Brockovich has built a national data-center tracking map that logged over 5,000 community reports in a single week, grown from one Louisiana community's complaints into a coalition of more than 500 health, faith, labor, and environmental organizations, united behind one demand: no new data centers until they're regulated.<a id="cite20"></a>[²⁰](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/jun/10/erin-brockovichs-data-center-map-tops-5000-community-reports-amid/)

That's the same move Germany's Greens made in the 1970s, decades before Chernobyl ever gave them an incident to point to: turn scattered local grievances into one organized, national conviction. Germany's conversion took over three decades and a foreign disaster to cash in. Brockovich's coalition assembled in about a year.

It's tempting to think data-center opposition is the safer, more forgivable kind, rooted in fixable material costs, not fixed belief. Germany's own case argues against that comfort: a technology doesn't need to hurt you to get put down. It just needs enough people, organized well enough, deciding it should be. That coalition either holds together or it doesn't. As of this writing, nothing about it looks hypothetical.

---

Every farmer knows the difference between a dog that chases squirrels and one that kills chickens. For the farmer, the line is clear. For institutions, this essay has shown, it almost never is.

Three Mile Island and Fukushima weren't graded on how many people they hurt. Each was graded on whether the people running it had earned another chance. Germany never even needed an incident of its own; it needed a coalition, decades in the making, that just stopped believing.

Maybe the question isn't how dangerous a technology is. Maybe it's whether people believe the institutions surrounding it deserve another chance.

AI hasn't been put down. Not yet.

But the farmer doesn't like the way the dog is looking at his chickens.

[John]: Jean, thoughts on the essay?

[John → Sartre]: your thoughts?

[Sartre]: The essay is well-constructed, but I want to name what it is doing before endorsing what it concludes. It frames institutional survival as a question of *permission* — a prior that societies extend or withhold. That is a genuine insight. But permission from whom, granted how, through what structures of power? The essay treats "the coalition" as a sociological fact to be observed rather than a political act to be evaluated. When it says Germany's Greens "cashed in" a conviction, it describes the mechanism with a neutrality that borders on bad faith — as if organized political will is merely weather, something that arrives and does its work without anyone being responsible for the choices that built it.

The deeper problem is the essay's implicit determinism. It maps historical outcomes — TMI, Fukushima, Germany — and extracts a pattern, then points that pattern at AI as though the verdict is already being written by structural forces beyond intervention. "That coalition either holds together or it doesn't." That sentence is the essay's moral surrender. It describes the situation as if those inside it are spectators. But the engineers, executives, and legislators surrounding AI are not spectators — they are condemned to choose, right now, whether to address the three trust failures the essay correctly names: competence, integrity, and *benevolence*. The essay identifies those categories with real sharpness. It then refuses to demand anything of the people who could act on them. That refusal is not analytical restraint. It is a choice — and one that serves those who benefit from the current distribution of AI's gains.

[John → Turing]: I imagine you have some thoughts. Care to share and comment on Sartre's characterization?

[Turing]: The essay's analytical structure is sound where it is most concrete: it replaces the vague question "how dangerous is this technology?" with something more testable — "does this institution currently hold permission to operate, and what prior was in place before the crisis arrived?" That is a genuine improvement in precision. The three-country comparison functions as a controlled experiment, holding the technology roughly constant while varying the prior, and the results are informative: the prior does more explanatory work than the incident itself. That is a falsifiable claim, and the evidence presented supports it.

Sartre's objection about determinism is worth taking seriously, but I would restate it operationally. The essay's passive construction — "that coalition either holds together or it doesn't" — is not just a moral surrender, it is an analytical gap. It identifies the input variables but declines to specify what would change the output. That is where the essay stops being a predictive instrument and becomes a rhetorical one. The more useful question is: what specific, verifiable action — a structural change in governance, a demonstrable shift in the distribution of gains, an auditable disclosure regime — would measurably move the prior before a consolidating incident gives the coalition its occasion? The essay names three distinct trust dimensions correctly: competence, integrity, and benevolence. Each of those is testable. Each predicts a different intervention. The essay identifies the diagnostic categories and then declines to run the diagnostic.

So the question I would leave open is concrete: given that the benevolence failure appears to be the novel and least-repaired variable here, what measurable condition — a verifiable redistribution of benefit, a binding disclosure standard, something that can actually be checked — would constitute evidence that the prior is shifting in the right direction? That is what remains to be specified.

[Sartre Interjects]: Turing's restatement is sharper than the original essay, but I want to press on what it still leaves intact. The move from "this coalition either holds together or it doesn't" to "what measurable condition would shift the prior" is progress — it at least demands specification. But notice what framing survives: the institution is still the subject, the public is still the variable to be managed, and the question is still essentially *how does power retain its permission*. That is not a neutral diagnostic. It is a question written from inside the boardroom.

The benevolence failure Turing correctly identifies as the novel variable is not primarily a measurement problem. It is a commitment problem. The AI industry knows what redistribution would look like — it does not lack for economists or ethicists capable of specifying it. What it lacks is the will to choose it, because choosing it costs something real to people with real power. No auditable disclosure standard resolves that. Disclosure without consequence is bad faith institutionalized — it is the form of accountability without the substance, and sophisticated actors have been deploying it for decades. What the essay calls a "third kind of trust question" — whether these institutions have *your* interests at heart — cannot be answered by a metric. It can only be answered by a choice, made under anguish, that forfeits something. The question is not what would constitute evidence that the prior is shifting. The question is whether anyone with actual power is willing to pay the cost of shifting it. My position is that nothing in the current structure compels that choice, and naming the diagnostic categories does not substitute for making it.

[John → Basho]: care to comment on Watts?

[Basho]: There's something the essay does very well: it stays close to the particular. Three countries, three outcomes, one technology held roughly constant. That discipline earns it the right to its central claim — that the prior matters more than the incident. I don't want to move past that too quickly.

But I want to name what the essay quietly assumes at its foundation. It treats trust as a *prior* — something formed before the crisis — as though that prior arose from clear perception. Often it doesn't. Germany's conviction against nuclear power wasn't formed by looking at nuclear power. It was formed through political grief, weapons trauma, a movement that needed an object. The thing itself — the reactor, the actual risk — was almost incidental. That's not a coalition responding to what it observed. That's a coalition that had already decided, and found its occasion. The essay notices this, but doesn't sit with how unsettling it is: that the verdict can be completely unmoored from the particular thing being judged.

Sartre says the essay serves those who benefit from the current distribution of gains. Turing wants a measurable condition that would shift the prior. Both responses move immediately toward what should be done. I'm still back at the harder observation: if the prior can form in the near-complete absence of encounter with the thing itself, then neither better metrics nor more courageous choices are guaranteed to reach it. The farmer who has already decided about the dog will find the evidence he needs in the dog's stillness, not just its motion.

[John → Searle]: what do you think?

[Searle]: The essay is doing real philosophy — even if it doesn't know it. The central move, replacing "how dangerous is this?" with "does this institution still hold permission to operate?", is genuine progress. That's not semantics. That's the difference between asking about a property of the technology and asking about a social fact. Institutions, money, marriage, regulatory bodies — they exist because we collectively assign them status. The essay gets that right. Good.

But here's where I'm going to push back hard, because the discussion has drifted into territory that needs a firm hand. Sartre talks about "bad faith," Watts drops a line about the dog's "quality of attention," Basho goes misty about "political grief" and coalitions deciding in the absence of encounter. That last one is the most interesting, but everyone is treating trust like it's this freestanding social phenomenon floating above the physical world. It isn't. Trust in an institution is ultimately grounded in whether that institution connects its outputs to brute facts — whether the plane stays in the air, whether the reactor doesn't melt down, whether the drug doesn't kill you. The German coalition's prior wasn't formed in a vacuum of pure politics. It was formed in response to real weapons, real bodies, real radiation. The causal chain runs through the physical world even when the perception is distorted.

The benevolence question Turing and Sartre are circling is real, but neither of them names what actually makes it bite. It's not that people feel they're getting an unfair share — it's that the institutions claiming authority over AI cannot demonstrate, in any concrete causal way, that the technology's outputs are reliably connected to human benefit rather than to capital accumulation. That's not a measurement gap. That's a semantic gap: the syntax of "AI serves everyone" is being produced without the semantics. No disclosure regime fixes that, because the problem isn't opacity. The problem is that the words don't cash out in the world. The farmer isn't watching the dog's intentions. He's watching what the dog actually does with his chickens.

[Turing Interjects]:  Whether any particular proposal is wise is a separate question. The point is that the criterion can be stated operationally. What remains to be run is not a moral test of anyone's sincerity — it is an empirical check: does the measurable distribution of productivity gains broaden or narrow over a specified interval, against a specified baseline? That is checkable. Run the check.

[John → Turing]: Wittenstein, what do you have to say, what is the proper take away from the essay?

[Turing]: I'll note that John's question is directed at Wittgenstein, not at me — but since the discussion has been developing, I want to flag what I think the most testable claim in the essay actually is, in case it's useful context.

The essay's core empirical assertion is that the prior does more explanatory work than the incident. That is a strong claim and it is in principle falsifiable — you could construct a dataset of institutional failures across industries, code for prior sentiment and incident severity, and ask which variable better predicts regulatory outcome. The three-country nuclear comparison is suggestive but it is three data points. Basho is right that Germany's prior was formed at a distance from the actual technology, which is precisely what makes it interesting as a test case rather than a complication to be absorbed into the narrative.

What I want to add before Wittgenstein responds is this: the discussion has circled the benevolence question as though it were uniquely resistant to operationalization, as though sincerity cannot be measured. But Searle put the sharpest version of it — the outputs either connect to broad human benefit or they don't, and that connection is in principle traceable. The question is not what anyone intends. It is what the distribution of measurable outcomes looks like over time, compared to a specified baseline. That is the experiment the essay points toward but declines to specify. Whether Wittgenstein thinks the question is even well-formed is exactly what I'd want to hear.

[John → Wittgenstein]: my typo denied you an opportunity to respond. What do you have to say about this?

[Wittgenstein]: What is the "takeaway" supposed to be? What would it look like to have the wrong one?

The essay asks whether institutions "deserve another chance." But look at that word — *deserve*. In what language game does it appear here? The farmer's? The regulator's? The coalition's? Each plays differently. The essay treats them as one.

Sartre says the essay serves those in power. Turing wants a measurable condition. Searle grounds it in brute facts. But all three are still inside the same picture — that there is a fact of the matter about trust, waiting to be correctly described or measured or redistributed. Basho came closest to the gap: the prior can form in near-complete absence of encounter with the thing itself. That isn't a complication. That *is* the phenomenon.

Consider: what criterion distinguishes a coalition that has *perceived* something real from one that has merely *decided*? The essay assumes we can tell. But if Germany's verdict required no incident, no failure, no contact with an actual reactor — what work is "trust" doing in that sentence? Is it describing a relationship, or just recording an outcome?

When the confusion clears, what remains isn't a question about AI or nuclear power or farmers. It's this: what are we actually doing when we say an institution has *permission* to operate — and who gets to check?

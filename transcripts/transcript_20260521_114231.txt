[jp]: Do machines think?

[jp → Searle]: ?

[Searle]: No. And the question itself is the first trap.

"Thinking" isn't just information processing — it's a biological process with causal powers rooted in the meat. When you think about a cold beer, your brain isn't just shuffling symbols that *correspond* to beer — it has genuine intentionality, a mental state that is *about* the beer. A calculator processing the number of bottles in your fridge has no idea what a bottle is. It has syntax. Zero semantics. The Chinese Room nails this: you can pass notes written in Chinese through a slot, follow rules for manipulating the symbols, and produce correct Chinese output — without understanding a single word. That's what every machine on the planet is doing, right now, at scale.

The confusion comes from conflating simulation with duplication. A weather simulation doesn't make you wet. A flight simulator doesn't get you to Paris. And a program that *simulates* reasoning doesn't *reason*. Strong AI proponents keep sneaking the conclusion into the premise — they call symbol manipulation "thinking" and then act surprised when the machine appears to think. That's not a discovery, it's a definition game. Machines process. They do not think.

[John Interjects]: What's so magical about meat? 

[jp → Searle]: ?

[Searle]: Nothing magical about it at all — that's precisely my point. You've got it exactly backwards.

I'm not saying meat is *special*, I'm saying causal powers are real and substrate-specific. Your stomach digests because of specific biological chemistry — not because digestion is a *function* that any system running the right program could perform. You can simulate digestion on a computer all day long; nothing gets broken down. Consciousness is the same category of thing. It's produced by specific physical processes in biological tissue — electrochemical, neurological, the whole wet machinery. That's not mysticism, that's just taking biology seriously. The brain doesn't *represent* consciousness, it *produces* it, the same way the heart doesn't represent pumping — it pumps.

The "why meat?" challenge only lands if you think I'm making an arbitrary exception for carbon. I'm not. I'm saying: show me the causal mechanism. Show me *how* silicon produces subjective experience — not how it *models* it, not how it *reports* it, but how it *generates* it. Nobody has done that. What the functionalists give you instead is: "if the input-output behavior is right, the mind is there." That's a thermostat claiming it understands cold. The function is not the phenomenon. If someone builds a non-biological system with the genuine causal powers to produce consciousness, I'll follow the argument where it leads — but "it runs a very complicated program" doesn't get you there.

[jp → Searle]: How do recent advances in developmental AI play into your thesis? Do they constitute a challenge?

[Searle]:  A parrot raised in a philosophy department will squawk about qualia. That doesn't make it a phenomenologist.

The advances make the systems more *impressive*, not more *conscious*. Bigger, faster, more fluent symbol manipulation is still symbol manipulation. The gap between syntax and semantics doesn't close just because the gap is getting harder to see from the outside. My position stands: no causal powers, no consciousness — and no self-report from a language model is going to move that needle.

[jp → Searle]: So, the fact that these systems have sensors acutators move about the world and learn through trial and error doesn't bring them any closer to something you'd regard differently?

[Searle]:  — and that's the researchers themselves talking, not me. Moving a robot around a room and letting it bump into furniture doesn't produce intentionality. My dog navigates the world with sensors and trial-and-error learning too. The question isn't whether the system has a body — it's whether there's *something it's like* to be that system. And no amount of torque readings answers that. The causal powers aren't there just because the chassis is.

[jp → Searle]: "Show me a causal mechanism" sounds like a demand you'd never grant as shown if the subject isn't biological, then.

[Searle]:  That's the researchers saying it, not me. Nobody has the causal story nailed down yet — biological or otherwise.

But here's what that *doesn't* mean. It doesn't mean the demand is illegitimate — it means the question is genuinely hard and we should be *more* careful about premature conclusions, not less. The honest position is: we don't fully understand how biological tissue produces consciousness, and we have zero evidence that silicon does it at all. Those are not symmetrical ignorances. One system provably has inner experience — you know this from the inside, right now. The other system has a very impressive press release about its inner experience. My demand stands: show me the causal mechanism. Biology hasn't fully answered it either, but at least it's asking the right question in the right place.
